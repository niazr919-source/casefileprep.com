There is no single number. Appeal response times are set by three overlapping sources - your policy, state insurance regulation and, for many health plans, federal rules - and they differ by line of insurance, plan type and urgency.

That sounds unhelpful, but it makes the task specific rather than vague: find the deadline that governs your appeal, write it down, and treat it as a date you will act on rather than a date you will wait through.

## Where to find the deadline that applies to you

**The denial letter.** Insurers generally state appeal rights and timeframes in the adverse decision itself, often near the end in smaller type. This is the single most useful paragraph in the document and the one most people skim past.

**Your policy or plan documents.** For health coverage, the summary plan description or evidence of coverage sets out the appeal process. For property and casualty, the policy wording governs, sometimes alongside an appraisal clause for disputes about the amount of loss rather than coverage.

**Your state department of insurance.** Every state regulates claims handling conduct, including how quickly insurers must acknowledge and respond to communications. State websites publish consumer guidance, and the national regulator directory lists each office.

**Federal rules, for many health plans.** Group health plans and individual market coverage commonly operate under defined federal timeframes for internal appeals and external review, with expedited routes for urgent situations. Whether a specific plan is covered depends on the plan type, and self-funded employer plans follow their own regime.

<Callout type="deadline" title="Track both clocks">
Two deadlines run at once and people watch only one. **Yours** is the window to file the appeal, stated in the denial letter and often strict. **Theirs** is the period within which they must respond. Note both in a calendar the day the denial arrives, with a reminder before each.
</Callout>

## Starting the clock properly

The response period generally runs from when the insurer **receives** the appeal, not when you send it. Two practical consequences:

**Send by a method that produces a delivery record.** Tracked or certified post with a signature is the strongest. Where an insurer offers a portal or a specific email address for appeals, use it and keep the confirmation.

**Record the delivery date, not the posting date.** Print the tracking record showing delivery, because tracking data is not retained online indefinitely.

If you submit through a portal, screenshot the confirmation screen including any reference number. Portals are convenient and their records are not always easy to retrieve later.

## While you wait

**Log everything.** One dated line per contact: who you spoke to, what was said, what was promised, what you sent and how. Insurance appeals run for weeks or months and involve several people at the insurer.

**Confirm calls in writing.** A short email afterwards - "as discussed today, you confirmed the appeal was received on 3 September and a decision is expected by 18 October" - creates a record without confrontation and frequently prompts accuracy.

**Do not keep resubmitting.** Sending the appeal three more times does not accelerate anything and muddies the record of when it was received.

**Keep gathering evidence** where the appeal turns on a factual dispute. An independent contractor's report, an engineer's assessment, a treating clinician's letter or a second opinion can be submitted as a supplement if it arrives during the review.

## When the deadline passes

**Follow up in writing immediately.** Reference the submission date, the delivery record, the applicable deadline and the fact that it has passed. Ask for a written status update and a decision date. Keep it factual and short.

**Escalate internally.** Ask for a supervisor or the appeals department manager, and put the same request to them in writing.

**Complain to the state department of insurance.** Regulators supervise insurer conduct and require companies to respond to consumer complaints, which creates a regulated record and sometimes unblocks a file that had stalled. Regulators generally do not adjudicate contract disputes or order payment of a particular claim - they oversee conduct - so treat this as an escalation alongside the appeal rather than a replacement for it.

**Check whether the delay itself has consequences.** Some states attach specific requirements to claims handling timeframes, and a failure to respond within them can matter separately from the merits. Whether that applies to your situation is a question for a licensed attorney in your state.

## After the internal decision

If the appeal succeeds, get the outcome in writing and confirm the payment or authorisation arrives as described.

If it fails, the final decision letter should set out what comes next - typically a second-level internal appeal where the plan provides one, then **external review** by an independent reviewer outside the insurer. For many health plans external review becomes available once internal appeals are exhausted, and the decision can bind the insurer. Availability for other lines depends on state law.

External review deadlines are usually short and stated in the final decision. Diarise that date the day the letter arrives, because it is one of the most commonly missed deadlines in the whole process.

For property claims, many policies also contain an **appraisal clause** for disputes about the amount of loss rather than coverage, under which each side appoints an appraiser and an umpire resolves differences. That is a separate route with its own mechanics. The [appeal documentation guide](/insurance-claim-denied-appeal-checklist/) covers how to structure the appeal itself and what evidence answers each type of denial.

## When to bring in a lawyer

This guide covers deadlines and process. It does not interpret your policy, determine whether an exclusion applies, assess whether a delay breached any legal standard, or advise on litigation.

Speak to a licensed attorney in your state if the amount is significant, if internal appeals have been exhausted without resolution, if the insurer alleges misrepresentation on your application, if a limitation period in the policy is approaching, if you are asked to sign a release, or if repeated deadlines pass without substantive response. Your state department of insurance can also explain the escalation routes available to you at no cost.
