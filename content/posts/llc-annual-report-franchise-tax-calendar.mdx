Forming an entity is a single event with a clear finish line. Keeping it alive is a calendar, and it is the part nobody warns you about at formation because the formation service has already been paid.

The obligations are individually small - a form, a fee, a return that is often zero. What makes them consequential is that they recur, they usually arrive with no reminder, and the penalty for forgetting compounds quietly until it surfaces at the worst moment: a loan application, a lease renewal, a licence, a sale.

## Annual reports

An annual report is an information filing, not a tax return. It confirms the state's record of who and where the entity is: name, principal address, registered agent and registered office, and in many states the members or managers.

Three things vary and all three matter:

**Whether your state requires one at all.** Some do not.

**How often.** Annual in most states that require it, biennial in several.

**When it is due.** Some states use a fixed calendar date for every entity; others tie the deadline to the anniversary of formation, which means your deadline is personal to your company and will not match anyone else's.

The fee is usually modest. The consequence of missing it is not.

## Franchise tax is a different thing entirely

The name causes real confusion. Franchise tax has nothing to do with franchising, and it is not income tax.

It is generally a charge for the privilege of existing as a registered entity in a state, and states calculate it in different ways - on gross revenue, on capital or net worth, or as a flat annual amount. Some states have no franchise tax; some have both franchise and income tax.

Two points catch people out.

**It can be due with no profit.** Where the charge is based on revenue or capital rather than income, a business that lost money can still owe it.

**Filing and paying are separate obligations.** Several states set a no-tax-due threshold below which nothing is payable, but still require a return to be filed confirming that. Businesses that owe nothing routinely assume there is nothing to do and fall out of good standing over a zero return.

<Callout type="warning" title="Two agencies, two deadlines">
The Secretary of State handles the annual report. The state revenue or comptroller office handles franchise tax. They are different agencies, with different portals, different deadlines and no obligation to tell each other anything. Filing one does not satisfy the other, and being current with one does not protect good standing if the other has lapsed.
</Callout>

## What losing good standing actually costs

The consequences are graduated, and the early stages are invisible until someone looks.

**Late fees and penalties**, which accrue.

**Loss of good standing.** Publicly visible on the state entity search. Checked by banks opening or reviewing accounts, lenders underwriting credit, landlords assessing a tenant, insurers, licensing bodies, larger clients running vendor checks, and any buyer conducting diligence. Some contracts require good standing as a condition.

**Restricted access to the courts.** In several states an entity that is not in good standing has a limited ability to bring or maintain a lawsuit until it is reinstated - which is a serious problem if you need to enforce a contract.

**Administrative dissolution or revocation.** The state terminates the entity for persistent failure to file. That is not the same as a clean wind-up: it leaves loose ends, and the liability shield you formed the entity for becomes considerably harder to rely on.

**Losing the name.** Once dissolved, the entity name may become available for someone else to register.

Reinstatement is usually possible, and usually involves back filings, accumulated penalties and weeks of processing.

## Multi-state businesses multiply everything

If the entity has registered to transact business in another state - foreign qualification - that state has its own annual report, its own franchise tax and its own registered agent requirement. None of it is covered by being current at home.

This is the most commonly forgotten category, particularly after a business stops operating in a state but never withdraws the registration. Obligations continue accruing in a state nobody is watching. The [foreign qualification guide](/foreign-qualification-llc-another-state/) covers when registration is triggered and how to withdraw cleanly.

## Do not rely on being reminded

Some states send a courtesy notice. Some do not. Notices go to the registered agent or registered office address, so they fail silently whenever that address is stale - which is exactly the situation where the entity is already drifting out of compliance.

A commercial registered agent typically forwards these and sends its own reminders, which is a real part of what the fee buys. If you act as your own agent, that job is yours, and the [registered agent guide](/registered-agent-requirements-explained/) covers what maintaining the role actually requires.

<Callout type="tip" title="Set the reminders further out than feels necessary">
Thirty days ahead, not seven. Filings get rejected for small errors, information takes time to gather, and expedited processing is not always available. A month of slack turns a rejection into an inconvenience rather than a lapse.
</Callout>

## Where a professional is worth the fee

This describes the shape of the obligations. It does not tell you what your state requires of your entity, how franchise tax is calculated on your numbers, or what to do about filings already overdue.

Speak to a CPA or a licensed tax adviser about franchise tax, income tax classification and payroll obligations - these are calculation questions with real money attached. Speak to a licensed business attorney if the entity has already been administratively dissolved, if you are unsure whether activity in another state triggers registration, or if a lapse has surfaced during a financing or sale process. Verify every date directly with the Secretary of State and the state revenue office rather than relying on a general guide.
