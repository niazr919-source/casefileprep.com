Disputes are rarely one-directional. A contractor sues for the unpaid balance; the homeowner says the work was defective and cost money to put right. A landlord sues for unpaid rent; the tenant says the deposit was never returned. In both cases there are two claims, and only one of them has been filed.

A counterclaim is how the second one gets heard - in the same case, on the same day, by the same judge. It is usually simpler and cheaper than filing separately, and it comes with a deadline that is easy to miss.

## When a counterclaim makes sense

Three conditions usually need to hold.

**You have a genuine claim.** Not a grievance about how the other party behaved, but something they owe you or a loss they caused, that you can put a figure on and evidence.

**It arises from the same dispute.** Most small claims systems require a counterclaim to come out of the same transaction or occurrence. A dispute about the same job, the same contract or the same tenancy is straightforward. Something entirely unrelated may have to be brought separately.

**It is worth the fee and the effort.** A counterclaim adds a filing fee, a service step and preparation. For a small amount, that arithmetic sometimes does not work.

Where those hold, raising it as a counterclaim rather than a separate case is usually the better route: one hearing, one set of evidence, one judge who sees the whole dispute rather than half of it.

## The deadline is the part people miss

This is the single most common way a counterclaim is lost.

Many courts require a counterclaim to be **filed and served on the claimant a set number of days before the hearing**, so the other side has notice and can prepare. That deadline can fall well before the hearing date printed on the papers.

Miss it and the counterclaim may simply not be heard. The original claim proceeds, is decided, and your claim has to be brought as a separate case afterwards - with its own fee and its own delay.

Find the deadline in the papers you were served, or ring the clerk. Then work backwards from it, because filing usually requires the form, the fee and arranging service.

<Callout type="deadline" title="Work backwards from the deadline, not forwards from today">
Filing a counterclaim involves the same three steps as filing a claim: complete the form, pay the fee, arrange service. Service in particular takes days and is not entirely within your control. Starting a week before the deadline is comfortable; starting the day before usually is not.
</Callout>

## Filing it

The mechanics mirror filing a claim, because that is essentially what it is.

**Get the right form.** Courts publish a counterclaim form, sometimes called a claim of defendant or a cross-claim depending on the jurisdiction. Use the court's own current version rather than a template site.

**Name the parties correctly.** The claimant becomes the party you are claiming against. Use their exact legal name - if they are a registered entity, the name on the state entity search rather than the trading name. The [legal name guide](/find-correct-legal-name-for-claim-form/) covers how to establish it.

**State the amount and how it is calculated.** A figure with a visible derivation is far stronger than a round number. Repair invoices, replacement costs, independent quotes.

**Describe the claim in a sentence or two.** What they did or failed to do, and what it cost you.

**Pay the fee**, or apply for a waiver if the cost is a barrier - the procedure is generally the same as for a claim.

**Serve it on the claimant.** Counterclaims usually have to be served, and the same rules about who may serve apply. The [service guide](/how-to-serve-court-papers-proof-of-service/) covers the methods and the proof of service that follows.

## What happens if it exceeds the limit

Small claims courts cap the amount they can award. If your counterclaim is worth more than the cap, jurisdictions handle it differently:

- **Waive the excess** and keep everything in small claims, accepting that you cannot recover above the cap.
- **Transfer the case** to a higher court where the full amount can be heard, which brings heavier procedure, higher costs and often the need for representation.
- **Split it**, which some courts expressly prohibit and which can bar the unclaimed part entirely.

That last option is the dangerous one. Deliberately splitting a single claim into two to stay under a limit is not permitted in many jurisdictions, and attempting it can cost you the remainder permanently.

Which route is available and which is sensible depends on the amounts, the procedural rules and how much complexity you are willing to take on. This is a genuine decision rather than an administrative one, and it is worth a conversation with a licensed attorney before choosing.

## How it changes the hearing

You now have two jobs rather than one: answering their claim, and presenting yours.

Practically, that means two sets of evidence and two one-minute summaries. Keep them separate in your own mind and in your exhibit index, because merging them produces a muddle that helps nobody. The judge is deciding two questions.

It also changes the arithmetic of the outcome. A judge may find for the claimant on their claim and for you on yours, then set one against the other - so the practical result is a net figure. Both claims can succeed, both can fail, or either can succeed alone.

<Callout type="tip">
Counterclaims prompt settlement more often than claims do, because both sides suddenly have something to lose. Decide before the hearing what net figure you would accept in either direction, so any corridor conversation happens from a position you have already thought through rather than under pressure.
</Callout>

## If you miss the deadline

Not necessarily fatal to the claim itself, but it usually means it cannot be heard in this case. Options generally reduce to bringing it as a separate small claims case afterwards, which costs another fee and another wait, and means the two halves of one dispute are decided by different judges on different evidence.

Ask the clerk whether a late counterclaim can be accepted before assuming it cannot - some courts have discretion, particularly where the claimant would not be prejudiced.

## Where to take advice

This covers the mechanics of raising a claim inside an existing case. It does not tell you whether your counterclaim is legally sound, what it is worth, whether to waive an excess or seek a transfer, or how a limitation period affects it.

Speak to a licensed attorney in your jurisdiction if the counterclaim exceeds the small claims limit, if a transfer to a higher court is being considered, if the dispute involves tenancy, employment or construction rules with their own statutory framework, or if the amounts justify professional input. Court self-help centres can explain the local counterclaim procedure and deadlines without charge.
