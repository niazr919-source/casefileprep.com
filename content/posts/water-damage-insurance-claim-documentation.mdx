Water damage claims are among the most commonly disputed in home insurance, and the reason is rarely dishonesty on either side. It is that water damage is unusually hard to evidence after the fact. Within forty-eight hours the visible evidence has been removed by drying equipment, the damaged materials are in a skip, and the question of whether the loss was sudden or gradual has become a matter of competing recollections.

The claims that resolve quickly are the ones where someone spent twenty minutes documenting before the cleanup started. This guide covers what to capture, in what order, and how to keep the file through what is often a months-long process. Policy wording governs everything here, so read your own policy alongside it.

## Step 1: Stop the source, then document before you clear

The order matters and the two duties can feel contradictory. Policies generally impose a duty to mitigate - to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage - and failing to do so can reduce or jeopardise a claim. At the same time, the evidence of what happened is physically present only until the cleanup begins.

The practical sequence resolves it:

1. **Make it safe.** Water and electricity together are dangerous. If water is near outlets, wiring or a panel, shut off power at the breaker or call an electrician. Leave the property if there is any structural concern.
2. **Stop the source.** Shut off the water at the main, close the relevant valve, or place containers under an active leak.
3. **Document, thoroughly and fast.** Ten to twenty minutes of photography and video before anything moves.
4. **Then mitigate.** Extract standing water, start fans and dehumidifiers, move undamaged contents out of harm's way.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not wait for the adjuster before drying">
A common and costly misunderstanding is that nothing should be touched until an adjuster inspects. Waiting days while materials stay wet invites secondary damage such as mould growth, and that additional damage may not be covered because it resulted from a failure to mitigate. Document first, then dry.
</Callout>

## Step 2: What to photograph

Photograph far more than seems necessary. Storage is free; a second chance is not.

**The source.** The burst pipe, failed hose, appliance connection, roof penetration or overflowing fixture. If a component failed, photograph it closely from several angles, and keep the failed part itself.

**The water at its extent.** Standing water before extraction, with something in frame for scale. Video walking through the affected area, narrating what is visible, captures relationships between rooms that stills miss.

**Water lines and staining.** The height water reached on walls, cabinets and furniture legs. Use a tape measure in frame.

**Affected building materials.** Flooring, subfloor, drywall, insulation, skirting, cabinetry, ceilings below the affected area.

**Contents.** Every damaged item, individually, before it is moved or discarded. Include serial numbers and labels where visible.

**Undamaged areas.** Photographs establishing the boundary of the damage prevent later disagreement about scope.

**Ongoing conditions.** Photograph again during drying and after materials are removed, so the progression is documented.

Keep original files with their metadata intact. Do not edit, crop or re-save through a messaging app, which strips the date information that makes the photograph useful.

## Step 3: Preserve physical evidence

Photographs of a failed hose are good; the failed hose in a labelled bag is better. Where the cause may be disputed - a failed appliance connector, a corroded fitting, a manufacturing defect - the component itself may matter to the insurer's investigation and to any recovery against a manufacturer or contractor.

Keep a section of damaged carpet, a piece of the removed drywall, or a sample of affected flooring where it is practical to do so. Label each with the date and the location it came from. Store it somewhere dry and out of the way rather than in the pile going to the skip.

## Step 4: Notify the insurer and start the log

Report promptly. Most policies require prompt notice, and delay can create a coverage argument entirely separate from the merits of the loss.

When you report, record the claim number, the adjuster's name and direct contact, what coverages apply, the deductible, what documentation they want next, and any deadline attached. Ask them to confirm the key points in writing.

Then start a claim log and keep it from that day. One dated line per event: every call, who you spoke to, what was said, what was promised; every document sent and how; every payment received. Confirm significant conversations by email afterwards with a short "as discussed" summary. Water claims frequently run for months and involve several people at the insurer, and the log is what keeps the account straight.

<Callout type="tip" title="From the file room">
Photograph the water meter or the utility bill if a leak ran undetected. Consumption data can establish when a leak began, which is directly relevant to the sudden-versus-gradual question and is one of the few genuinely objective pieces of evidence available.
</Callout>

## Step 5: Build the contents inventory as you go

Reconstructing an inventory from memory weeks later is the single most tedious part of a water claim and produces the weakest result - which is the argument for [building one before you need it](/home-inventory-for-insurance-claims/). Building it as items are handled costs little extra.

For each damaged item record: description, brand and model, age or approximate purchase date, original cost, where purchased, current condition, and a photograph. Group by room.

For proof of ownership and value, receipts are best. Where they no longer exist, card and bank statements, order confirmation emails, warranty registrations, and earlier photographs showing the item in the home all serve. Manuals and packaging retained in the loft are also useful.

Understand [which valuation basis your policy uses](/actual-cash-value-vs-replacement-cost/), because it changes the figures substantially. **Replacement cost** pays what it costs to replace with a comparable new item. **Actual cash value** pays replacement cost less depreciation. Many policies pay actual cash value initially and release the remainder once the item is actually replaced and receipts are submitted - which means keeping the replacement receipts is part of getting fully paid.

## Step 6: The sudden versus gradual question

This distinction decides more water claims than any other single issue, so build your evidence around it from the start.

Many homeowner policies cover water damage that is sudden and accidental while excluding damage that developed over time through seepage, repeated leaking or deferred maintenance. An insurer assessing the claim will look at when the problem started, when it was noticed, and what was done about it.

What helps: a clear timeline written the day of the event, evidence of when the area was last seen dry, maintenance and service records for the failed component, prior inspection reports, and utility consumption data. What hurts: a gap between noticing a problem and acting on it, and messages or notes that suggest the issue had been known for some time.

Record the timeline honestly and early. A truthful account written on day one is far more credible than a carefully constructed one produced after a coverage question is raised.

## Step 7: Through the adjustment

Be present for the adjuster's inspection if you can, and walk them through your documentation rather than leaving them to find things. Point out damage that is not obvious - under flooring, inside cabinets, in the ceiling below.

When the estimate arrives, read it line by line against your own inventory and estimates. Check that every room and every item appears, that the scope of repair is realistic, and that the valuation basis matches your policy. Where you disagree, respond in writing, item by item, with your evidence attached. Independent contractor estimates carry more weight than assertion.

Keep every receipt for replacements, because replacement cost policies often hold back the depreciation until the replacement actually happens.

## When to consult a lawyer

This guide covers documentation. It does not interpret your policy, determine whether an exclusion applies, assess whether a settlement offer is reasonable, or advise on how to challenge a denial.

Speak to a licensed attorney in your state if the claim is denied - the [appeal process](/insurance-claim-denied-appeal-checklist/) has its own deadlines - if the insurer relies on a gradual-damage or maintenance exclusion you dispute, if the settlement is substantially below your documented estimates, if the property is uninhabitable and loss-of-use cover is being refused, or if you are asked to sign a release you do not fully understand. Your state department of insurance also accepts consumer complaints and can explain what the regulator expects of insurers, at no cost.
