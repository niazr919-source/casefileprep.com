An LLC exists under the laws of the state that formed it. Cross a state line and do business there, and that second state generally wants to know about it - and wants its own filings, its own fees and its own registered agent.

This is foreign qualification. "Foreign" means out-of-state, not out-of-country, which is the first thing that confuses people. The second is that the test for when it applies is genuinely fuzzy, and the states that draw it most clearly do so by listing what does *not* count.

## What usually triggers it

There is no single national test, but the factors states weigh are broadly consistent. Registration is commonly required where the entity has:

- a **physical location** in the state - office, shop, warehouse, workshop;
- **employees** working there;
- **inventory or equipment** stored there;
- a **regular, repeated course of business** rather than isolated transactions;
- **real property** owned or leased there;
- a **licence** from that state to carry on a regulated activity.

Most state statutes then list activities that do **not**, on their own, constitute transacting business. Those lists commonly include holding bank accounts, maintaining an office solely for the entity's own internal affairs, being party to a lawsuit, collecting debts, selling through independent contractors, and conducting an isolated transaction completed within a short period.

## Where it gets genuinely uncertain

Two situations account for most of the real-world doubt.

**Selling online to customers in other states.** A business shipping to customers nationwide, with no presence anywhere else, is generally not qualifying in fifty states - and nobody sensible suggests it should. But add a warehouse, a fulfilment arrangement that stores your inventory in another state, or a physical presence of any kind, and the analysis changes. Note that sales tax nexus is a *separate* question with its own rules; being required to collect sales tax in a state does not automatically mean you must foreign-qualify there, and vice versa.

**Remote employees.** This is now the most common way a small business ends up qualifying somewhere it never planned to. Hiring one person who works from home in another state commonly triggers registration there, alongside payroll tax registration and employment obligations under that state's law.

Neither of these has a clean general answer, which is precisely why they are worth asking an attorney and an accountant about rather than resolving by assumption.

<Callout type="warning" title="The penalty that actually matters">
Back fees and penalties are irritating. The consequence with teeth is that many states restrict an unregistered foreign entity from bringing or maintaining a lawsuit in their courts until it registers. A business that cannot enforce its own contracts or chase its own debts in a state where it trades has a real problem - and it usually discovers this at the moment it needs to sue.
</Callout>

## What registration involves

The filing is usually called an **application for certificate of authority** or **application for registration**, and it typically requires:

- the entity's **name as registered at home**, plus an alternative name if the original is unavailable in the new state;
- a **certificate of good standing** from the home state, often required to be recent;
- a **registered agent and registered office in the new state** - the same continuous requirement as at home;
- the **formation date and home jurisdiction**;
- a **filing fee**.

The name point catches people. If your name is already taken in the new state, you generally have to register under an assumed or alternative name there, and then trade under that name in that state.

## Then it never stops

This is the part worth understanding before deciding.

Every state where the entity is registered brings its **own recurring obligations**: annual report, franchise tax or equivalent, registered agent maintenance, and any local licensing. None of it is covered by being current at home.

Register in three states and you have four compliance calendars. The [compliance calendar guide](/llc-annual-report-franchise-tax-calendar/) covers how to build one that does not depend on being reminded.

## Withdrawing when you leave

Stopping trading in a state does not end anything. The registration stands, and with it the annual report, the franchise tax and the registered agent requirement - accruing quietly in a state nobody is watching, until penalties or an administrative revocation surface years later.

Ending it properly means filing a **certificate of withdrawal** or **cancellation**, which in several states requires tax clearance from the state revenue office first. Request that early, because it takes weeks.

This is among the most commonly forgotten obligations in small business compliance, and it is also one of the easiest to avoid: withdraw when you leave, and keep the accepted filing with the company records. The [dissolution guide](/how-to-dissolve-an-llc-checklist/) covers the same point for closing an entity entirely, where unwithdrawn foreign registrations are the classic loose end.

<Callout type="tip">
Before expanding into a new state, price the whole thing rather than just the registration fee: filing, registered agent, annual report, franchise tax and any local licence, every year. It is usually modest, but it is recurring, and knowing the figure makes the decision a decision rather than a surprise.
</Callout>

## When to ask rather than guess

Whether your activity in a particular state amounts to transacting business is a legal question with a state-specific answer, and it is one of the areas where confident guesses are most often wrong.

Speak to a licensed business attorney before hiring an employee in another state, before opening any physical location, if you are already trading somewhere without being registered, or if you need to sue in a state where the entity is not qualified. Speak to a CPA about state tax registration, payroll obligations and sales tax nexus, which run on separate rules from the ones described here.
