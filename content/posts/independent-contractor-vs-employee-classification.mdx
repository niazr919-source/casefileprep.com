The agreement says independent contractor. Both sides signed it. Neither party is confused about what they intended.

None of that decides the question. Classification follows the working relationship as it actually operates, and it is assessed by more than one authority, each applying its own test for its own purpose. A worker can be a contractor for one and an employee for another, from the same set of facts.

That is the part worth understanding before anything else, because it explains why confident answers in this area are so often wrong.

## Why more than one answer is possible

Different laws exist to do different jobs, so each brings its own test.

**The IRS** cares about employment taxes - withholding, Social Security, Medicare and unemployment tax. Its analysis rests on common law rules and centres on control.

**The Department of Labor** cares about minimum wage and overtime under the Fair Labor Standards Act. Its Wage and Hour Division published a final rule on 10 January 2024, effective 11 March 2024, revising how that analysis is conducted; the guidance sits in regulations at 29 CFR Part 795, and it replaced an earlier 2021 rule. Anything written before that date may describe a framework no longer applied.

**Your state** cares about unemployment insurance, workers compensation and state wage law, and sets its own test for each. Several states apply notably stricter standards than the federal position, and a few treat most workers as employees unless specific conditions are met.

The practical consequence: satisfying one test is not a defence under another. A business can be correct with the IRS and exposed under state wage law simultaneously.

<Callout type="warning" title="The contract is evidence, not a decision">
A written agreement describing someone as an independent contractor matters - it shows what both parties understood the arrangement to be, and the IRS treats the type of relationship as one of its three evidence categories. But it is weighed alongside how the work is actually controlled, paid for and integrated into the business. Where the paperwork and the working reality diverge, the reality carries the weight. Drafting a stronger contract does not fix a relationship that functions as employment.
</Callout>

## What the IRS weighs

The IRS asks whether the business has the right to control how the work is done, and organises the evidence into three groups.

**Behavioural control.** Whether the business directs the manner of the work rather than only the result. Instructions on when, where and in what sequence to work; required tools or premises; specified methods; training in a particular way of doing things - all point towards employment. A contractor is typically engaged for an outcome and decides how to reach it.

**Financial control.** Whether the worker bears economic risk. Significant investment in their own equipment, unreimbursed expenses, services made available to the wider market, payment calculated by the job rather than by the hour, and a genuine possibility of profit or loss all point towards contractor status.

**Type of relationship.** Written agreements, whether employee-type benefits are provided, whether the engagement is open-ended rather than for a defined project, and whether the services performed are a key part of the ordinary business.

No factor is decisive. The IRS considers all evidence of the degree of control and independence together, and the weighting shifts with the facts.

## Asking rather than assuming

Where a relationship is genuinely borderline and the amounts involved are meaningful, guessing is the expensive option.

**Form SS-8** asks the IRS to determine a worker status for federal employment tax purposes. Either the business or the worker can file it. It takes time, and the determination reflects the facts as described - so describing them accurately matters more than describing them favourably. Speak to a CPA or a tax adviser before filing.

For wage and hour questions, the Department of Labor publishes a fact sheet, frequently asked questions and a small entity compliance guide alongside the regulations. For state questions, the state labour department and the state unemployment insurance agency are the authorities, and they are separate from each other.

## What it costs to get wrong

Misclassification is treated seriously because a misclassified employee may miss minimum wage and overtime protections, along with other benefits and protections provided by law.

Exposure can include back employment taxes with interest and penalties, liability for unpaid wages and overtime, assessments under state unemployment and workers compensation schemes, and claims relating to benefits never provided. Because separate agencies apply separate tests, a single arrangement can generate liability in more than one place at once.

There is a further practical dimension worth noting: engaging a worker in another state can trigger registration and payroll tax obligations there, which is one of the more common ways a small business finds itself [qualifying to do business somewhere it never planned to](/foreign-qualification-llc-another-state/).

## Where the drift happens

Classifications that were defensible at the start often stop being so, and nobody notices because nothing was formally changed.

A contractor engaged for a three-month project is still there two years later. The scope has expanded until they do whatever is needed. They attend the weekly meeting, they use a company laptop, they have no other clients, and the invoices arrive on a monthly cycle for a consistent amount.

Nothing in that sequence was a decision. The relationship simply became employment while the paperwork stayed put. Reviewing long-running engagements periodically against the actual working arrangement is the cheapest safeguard available - considerably cheaper than the same review conducted by an agency after a complaint.

<Callout type="tip">
Test the arrangement with a single question: if this person told you tomorrow that they would deliver the agreed result but in their own way, on their own schedule, using their own equipment, would that be acceptable? If the answer is no, control is sitting with the business, and the classification deserves a proper look.
</Callout>

## When to take advice

This describes the shape of the tests. It does not tell you how a particular worker should be classified, which is fact-specific and governed by several separate standards at once.

Speak to a CPA or a licensed tax adviser about federal employment tax treatment and before filing Form SS-8. Speak to a licensed attorney in your state who handles employment matters before engaging workers in a new state, if a worker has questioned their classification, if you are contacted by a labour or unemployment agency, or if a long-running contractor engagement has grown into something closer to employment. Your state labour department can confirm which test it applies, and the Department of Labor publishes the current federal wage and hour guidance directly.
