Security deposit disputes are among the most common matters in small claims courts, and among the most winnable on documentation alone. The amounts are usually within the small claims limit, the legal question is narrow, and the outcome very often turns on a single issue: who can show the condition of the property at the beginning and at the end of the tenancy.

That makes deposit disputes unusual. Most of the work is done before there is a dispute, by whichever party took photographs on the day they got the keys. This guide covers what to gather, when the statutory clocks start, how to read an itemised deduction statement, and how to assemble the claim file. It is written for the general US framework; deposit rules are set by state law and differ significantly, so verify against the statute for the state where the property sits.

## Step 1: Establish the condition at move-in

The strongest deposit file starts on the first day of the tenancy, but useful evidence usually exists even when nobody planned ahead.

The ideal record is a dated set of photographs or video covering every room, floors, walls, appliances, fixtures, window coverings and any pre-existing damage, taken before furniture arrives, together with a signed move-in condition checklist retained by both parties. Where a checklist exists, it typically carries considerable weight because both sides agreed to it at a time when neither was in dispute.

Where none of that exists, assemble substitutes: the original listing photographs, the lease and any condition addendum, any email or message in which you reported a problem shortly after moving in, maintenance requests logged during the tenancy, and the recollection of anyone who helped you move.

<Callout type="tip">
Photographs are far more useful when something in frame fixes the date. A newspaper, a phone screen showing the date, or simply keeping the original files with their metadata intact all work. Screenshots of photographs strip that metadata; always preserve the originals.
</Callout>

## Step 2: Document the move-out properly

Move-out evidence is the half of the record you can still control, so treat the last hour in the property as an evidence exercise.

Photograph or film every room after it is empty and cleaned, in the same order as your move-in record so the two sets can be compared frame by frame. Capture the areas that are commonly disputed: carpets, walls, oven interior, refrigerator, bathroom grout and sealant, window tracks, blinds, and any wall fixings. Photograph areas that are undamaged as well, because a complete record makes a later claim about a specific spot easier to assess.

Then record the handover itself: the date and time keys were returned, to whom, and how. If a joint walkthrough happens, note who attended and what was said, and follow up the same day with a short email summarising it. If your state gives tenants the right to request a pre-move-out inspection, exercise it - it can convert a surprise deduction into a repairable item.

## Step 3: Provide a forwarding address, in writing

This small administrative step causes a disproportionate number of failed claims. Deposit statutes commonly require the landlord to send the deposit or the itemised statement to the tenant's last known address or a forwarding address the tenant supplied.

Send the forwarding address in writing, keep a copy, and use a method that produces a delivery record. A landlord who can show they posted the statement to the only address they held is in a much stronger position than one who cannot, and a tenant who never supplied an address weakens their own argument that nothing arrived.

## Step 4: Know the clocks

Two deadlines matter and both are set by state law.

The **return deadline** is the period after the tenancy ends within which the landlord must return the deposit, send an itemised statement of deductions, or both. It commonly falls somewhere between two weeks and two months.

The **filing deadline** is the limitation period for bringing a claim, which is generally much longer but is absolute. If a deposit dispute is approaching its limitation period, that is a point to get advice rather than to keep negotiating.

Some states attach consequences to a landlord missing the return deadline, which can include losing the right to claim deductions or exposure to additional damages. Those provisions vary widely and several have specific conditions attached, so read the statute rather than relying on a general description of it.

## Step 5: Read the itemised statement critically

When an itemised deduction statement arrives, work through it line by line rather than reacting to the total.

For each deduction, ask four questions. Is the item damage or is it wear and tear? Is the charge supported by a receipt, invoice or estimate, or is it a round number with no derivation? Does the item appear in the move-in record as pre-existing? And is the amount proportionate, given the age and expected life of what was replaced - a five-year-old carpet replaced entirely, charged at full price, is a common point of dispute.

| Commonly disputed deduction | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Full carpet replacement | Age of carpet, whether cleaning would suffice, depreciation applied |
| Repainting the whole unit | Length of tenancy, whether damage exceeds ordinary marks |
| Blanket cleaning fee | Whether the state permits it, whether an itemised cost is shown |
| Nail holes and wall fixings | Number and size, and whether the lease permitted hanging items |
| Appliance repair | Age, whether the fault is use or failure, service invoice |

Build a table of your own setting each deduction against your evidence and your position on it. That table becomes the core of both your demand letter and your [exhibit index](/small-claims-court-document-preparation-checklist/).

## Step 6: Send a demand that is easy to say yes to

A [deposit demand letter](/how-to-write-a-demand-letter-checklist/) works best when it is short, specific and free of grievance. Set out the tenancy dates, the deposit paid, the amount returned, the deductions disputed and why, the total requested, and a specific date for payment. Attach the evidence you rely on, and reference each deduction to its exhibit.

Landlords who receive a calm letter with photographs attached frequently settle, because the alternative is to attend a hearing carrying a weaker record. Landlords who receive four pages of accumulated frustration frequently do not, because the letter has told them nothing about the strength of the evidence.

Send it with a delivery record and keep everything together.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not withhold the last month's rent instead">
Treating the deposit as the final rent payment is a widespread instinct and, in many states, creates a separate and clearer liability than the deposit dispute you were trying to solve. Whether that step is lawful where you are is a legal question, and it is one worth asking before acting rather than afterwards.
</Callout>

## When a deposit dispute is not only about the deposit

This guide covers assembling evidence and understanding the process. It does not determine whether a specific deduction is lawful in your state, whether a statutory penalty applies to a late return, what your deposit claim is worth, or how deposit rules interact with a disputed eviction or unpaid rent.

Speak to a licensed attorney in the state where the property is located if there is a counterclaim for unpaid rent or damage beyond the deposit, if an eviction is part of the picture, if the amount exceeds the small claims limit, if a limitation period may be close, or if you are a landlord uncertain whether your deduction process meets statutory requirements. Many states also have tenant advice services, housing counselling agencies and court self-help centres that can explain local rules without charge.
