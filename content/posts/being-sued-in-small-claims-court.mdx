Almost everything written about small claims is written for the person filing. The person who opens the envelope has a different problem, a shorter timeline, and considerably more anxiety - and far less to read.

This guide is for that side. What the documents mean, which dates actually matter, what a defence looks like in practice, and the one decision that reliably makes things worse.

## What you have actually been given

The packet usually contains three things.

**The claim form.** Who is suing, who they are suing, how much they want, and a short statement of why. Read the "why" carefully - it is often only a sentence or two, and it defines what the hearing is about.

**A notice of hearing.** The date, time and location. In many small claims systems this is set when the case is filed, which is why there is no separate "response deadline" the way there is in higher courts.

**Instructions from the court.** Frequently ignored and frequently the most useful page. It will say whether a written response is required, whether counterclaims have a deadline, and what to bring.

Read all of it once, then again the next day. People routinely miss the hearing date on first reading because they are focused on the amount.

<Callout type="deadline" title="Two dates, not one">
There is the **hearing date**, and there may be a separate, earlier deadline for filing a written response or a counterclaim. Missing the second one does not always end the case, but it can cost you the ability to raise your own claim. Ring the clerk and ask what applies - it is a two-minute call and they answer it constantly.
</Callout>

## Check who is being sued

This matters more than it looks.

If you trade through a **registered company or LLC** and the claim names *you personally*, that may be an error worth raising - the entity and the owner are different legal persons. If the claim names the entity and you respond personally, that can create confusion about who is actually before the court.

Equally, if you are a **sole proprietor**, there is no separate entity: you and the business are the same legal person, and naming either is usually effective.

Check the name on the claim against reality before doing anything else.

## Was service valid?

Courts take service seriously because a judgment against someone who was never told is a serious thing. Rules vary, but service is commonly defective where:

- the papers were handed over by the **plaintiff personally**, where the rules require a neutral server;
- they were left with **someone who does not live or work** at the address;
- they were sent to an **address you had already left**;
- the **method used** is not one the court permits for that type of claim.

Being aware of a claim is not the same as having been properly served, though in practice many courts will not let a defendant who clearly knows about the case use a technical service defect to avoid it entirely.

If you think service was defective, that is a point to raise properly rather than to rely on by staying away. How and when depends on the jurisdiction, and it is worth asking a licensed attorney or a court self-help centre.

## What a defence actually looks like

Small claims defences tend to fall into a handful of shapes. Working out which one you have makes preparation much easier.

**"It did not happen the way they say."** A factual dispute. Your evidence is the same four families the claimant is assembling: the agreement, the money, the communications, the loss. The [document preparation checklist](/small-claims-court-document-preparation-checklist/) applies to you as much as to them.

**"I already paid, or paid part."** Bank records, receipts, payment app history. This is the most straightforward defence to evidence and the most commonly under-documented.

**"The work was defective, or never delivered."** Photographs, independent quotes, messages reporting the problem at the time.

**"That is not what we agreed."** The contract, the quote, or the messages that set the terms.

**"They are suing the wrong person."** Wrong entity, wrong individual, or a debt that belongs to someone else.

**"It is too late."** Limitation periods apply to claims, and a claim brought after the period has expired may be barred. Whether that applies to your facts is a legal question, and a genuinely important one to raise before the hearing rather than after.

**"I agree, but not with the amount."** Perfectly respectable. Disputing quantum rather than liability is common and courts deal with it routinely.

<Callout type="warning" title="Partial payment can restart a clock">
In some jurisdictions, making a payment or acknowledging a debt in writing can restart a limitation period that had nearly expired. If a claim looks old and you are considering paying something to make it go away, that is worth checking first, because the act of paying can revive something that was otherwise unenforceable.
</Callout>

## Preparing, in practical terms

The preparation is a mirror image of the claimant's.

**Build a dated timeline.** Every event, in order, with what proves it. The [case timeline guide](/how-to-write-case-timeline-small-claims/) covers the method, and it works identically from the defence side.

**Assemble exhibits and number them.** Same rules on copies, originals and annotations - the [copies guide](/how-many-copies-evidence-small-claims-court/) covers what the court expects.

**Include the unhelpful facts.** A defence that ignores the claimant's best point invites the judge to assume you have no answer to it.

**Prepare a one-minute version.** What was agreed, what actually happened, what you dispute and why. Spoken, not read.

**Bring a witness if one exists**, and tell them the date, place and arrival time.

## Settling

A large share of small claims matters settle, and settling is not an admission that the claim was right - it is a decision that resolution is worth more than the fight.

If you do settle, three things belong in writing:

- **the amount and the payment date or schedule;**
- **that payment resolves the matter in full**, if that is the agreement;
- **what happens to the court case** - normally the claimant withdrawing it or the court dismissing it.

A verbal promise that "we will drop it" with no withdrawal filed leaves the case live and the hearing date standing. Get the disposal of the case recorded, not just the payment.

## If you also have a claim against them

Disputes are frequently two-sided. If the other party owes you something arising from the same matter, most small claims systems allow a counterclaim - but usually with its own deadline and often its own fee.

Raising it as a counterclaim keeps both halves in front of the same judge on the same day, which is generally simpler than filing separately. There is more on the mechanics in the [counterclaim guide](/how-to-file-a-counterclaim-small-claims/).

## Attend, even if you feel unprepared

The single most damaging choice available is not turning up.

A court can enter a **default judgment** against an absent defendant, and that judgment is enforceable in the ordinary ways: wage garnishment, bank levies, liens against property. It also becomes considerably harder to challenge afterwards than it would have been to defend in person - set-aside applications have deadlines, grounds, and no guarantee of success.

If you genuinely cannot attend on the date, contact the clerk **before** it, not after. Courts have a procedure for requesting a postponement, often with a fee and a deadline. A request made in advance is routine; an explanation offered after judgment is a much harder conversation.

## Where a lawyer earns their fee

This guide covers procedure and preparation. It does not tell you whether you have a defence, whether a limitation period has expired on your facts, whether service was legally defective, or what a settlement should be worth.

Speak to a licensed attorney in your jurisdiction if the amount is significant, if a limitation argument may apply, if the claim arises from an employment or tenancy relationship with its own statutory rules, if you are being sued personally over a business debt, or if a default judgment has already been entered. Court self-help centres and legal aid clinics can also explain local defence procedure at no cost, though they cannot assess the merits of your particular matter.
